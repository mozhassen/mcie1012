02 Course overview

This course equips students with a foundation in the principles and applications of civics and ethics. It covers the definitions of civics, ethics, morality, and amorality, and the historical development of civic and ethical education, alongside citizens' rights and responsibilities and the competencies of a good citizen.

Students work through the major ethical approaches: teleological ethics such as hedonism, ethical and psychological egoism, and utilitarianism; deontological ethics including duty, divine-based morality, Kant's categorical imperative, and W. D. Ross's prima facie duty; and virtue ethics, civic virtue, and non-normative ethics. Applied ethics is examined through development, environmental, and professional cases. The second half turns to the state, government, and citizenship, including constitutions, democracy, and human rights.

03 Course objectives

Upon successful completion of this course, students will have achieved the following:

  • Understand the origin and development of civics and ethical education.
  • Recognize the purpose and significance of civics and ethical education in society.
  • Identify and discuss the rights and responsibilities of citizens.
  • Explore different ethical approaches, including teleological and deontological ethics.
  • Analyze and apply ethical theories to real-world situations.
  • Examine applied ethics in areas such as development, environmental ethics, and professional ethics.
  • Evaluate ethical decision-making processes and moral judgments.
  • Understand the attributes and structures of the state and different forms of government.
  • Define citizenship, including ways of acquiring and losing citizenship, and its historical context in Ethiopia.
  • Explore state formation and nation-building in the Ethiopian context.
  • Comprehend the features and purposes of constitutions.
  • Discuss different forms of democracy and its fundamental values and principles.
  • Analyze the democratization process and the role of major actors.
  • Understand the nature of human rights, their protection, and oversight mechanisms.

09 Academic integrity, referencing & AI usage

Academic integrity. All submitted work, including tests and assignments, must be your own, in line with the AASTU Senate Legislation of 2017. Referencing is encouraged, but proper citation is essential; presenting external material as your own is plagiarism and is strictly prohibited, with consequences up to failing grades or dismissal from the course. Allowing someone to copy your work is treated the same as copying someone else's.

Referencing style. APA Style, 7th edition, is required for all citations and references in written work and presentations. Other styles need prior instructor approval.

Plagiarism detection. The university may use AI-detection and similarity-checking tools such as Turnitin. For final submissions: 0–20% similarity is generally acceptable, 21–30% raises concern and requires instructor review, and anything above 30% is disqualified with a zero mark. Similarity from any single source must stay under 5%.

AI usage. Generative AI may be used to assist with coursework, under two principles. Accountability: you're fully responsible for what you submit, including fact-checking and editing any AI output — submitting inaccurate or poorly reasoned work is your failure, not the AI's. Transparency: any assignment where AI was used must include an AI Usage Statement naming the tool(s) used, how they were used, and the key prompts if the contribution was significant. Leaving this out, or submitting AI-generated work undisclosed, is treated as a plagiarism violation.
